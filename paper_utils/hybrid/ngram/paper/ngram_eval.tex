\begin{quote}\small\noindent\textbf{Abstract.}
Comparing tokenizers currently means choosing between cheap intrinsic metrics with unreliable downstream correlation and GPU-scale pretraining sweeps. We propose a middle point: fit an interpolated modified Kneser--Ney $n$-gram language model to each tokenizer's own token stream and report held-out bits per UTF-8 byte (bpb), computable in CPU minutes. On nine closely matched 34.7K-vocabulary tokenizers with a 20-seed transformer sweep, where compression is at chance, $n$-gram bpb orders 85\% of the pairs both methods resolve ($p{=}0.0013$). On the 48-tokenizer TokEval suite at 128K vocabulary, held-out \emph{bigram} bpb reaches Spearman $\rho{=}0.80$ against published 1.27B-model validation bpb, exceeding every intrinsic metric reported there, including R\'enyi efficiency (0.57) and plug-in trigram entropy (0.44, n.s.). Our central observation is a coverage confound that governs when sequential proxies work at all: the $n$-gram's context is measured in \emph{tokens}, so when candidate tokenizers differ in fertility, higher orders reward fragmentation rather than quality---at $n{=}3$ the panel's second-\emph{worst} tokenizer downstream is scored fourth-\emph{best}. The usable order is the largest $n$ at which held-out $n$-gram coverage is comparable across candidates, a diagnostic the metric provides about itself.
\end{quote}\vspace{4pt}}]

\section{Introduction}
Tokenizer evaluation is polarized. Intrinsic metrics---compression, fertility, R\'enyi efficiency \cite{zouhar2023}, Zipf statistics \cite{lotz2025}---cost seconds but correlate inconsistently with the models eventually trained \cite{schmidt2024,cognetta2024,ali2024}. Extrinsic evaluation pretrains a model per tokenizer \cite{meister2026,lotz2025}, which is what practitioners iterating on a tokenizer cannot afford. Between plug-in corpus statistics and a transformer sits an old, unfashionable object: a smoothed $n$-gram language model. We show that, used carefully, it is a surprisingly strong tokenizer evaluation---and that ``carefully'' has exact content.

\textbf{Method.} For each candidate tokenizer we encode disjoint held-out and training text, fit an interpolated modified Kneser--Ney model \cite{chen1998} of order $n$ over the token stream (dense-prefix-id \texttt{numpy} counting; minutes on a laptop), and report total held-out bits divided by the \emph{true UTF-8 byte length of the text}, measured before tokenization. Three design points: (i)~the model is a proper distribution---level~1 interpolates into a uniform over the tokenizer's full alphabet, so bpb is a real code length and unused vocabulary is priced, not free; (ii)~the true-byte denominator makes scores comparable across tokenizers without per-token byte-accounting corrections, and a lossy tokenizer trips a round-trip check rather than being silently flattered; (iii)~the metric is deterministic, so uncertainty comes from a \emph{paired bootstrap over held-out documents}: every tokenizer scores the same documents, document difficulty cancels, and every pair of tokenizers gets a confidence interval.

\section{Fine distinctions: a matched panel}
We first test the regime where intrinsic metrics fail: nine tokenizers (BPE, Unigram, BPE-initialized Unigram variants, MinGram, MinGram-PP, PathPiece at two prune settings, ConvexTok) sharing a 34.7K vocabulary, corpus, and pretokenizer, each with $\geq$20 seeds of 1.27B-token nanochat \cite{karpathy2025} pretraining. Downstream val bpb separates 30/36 tokenizer pairs (Welch, $p{<}.05$); the CORE task benchmark separates only 9/36---its between-arm range is smaller than its seed noise, so at this scale \emph{the expensive benchmark is the one that cannot rank tokenizers}. The $n$-gram metric, from 5\,MB of held-out text, separates 31/36 pairs at 95\% and reproduces its ranking exactly on disjoint text at $n{\geq}3$ (Kendall $\tau{=}1.0$; $\tau{=}0.81$ below). On downstream-resolved pairs, $n{\geq}3$ bpb agrees with val bpb on 21/30 ($p{=}.021$; replicate 20/30); where \emph{both} metrics are individually confident, 17/20 = 85\% ($p{=}.0013$). Compression agrees on 17/30 and 16/30---chance. Multilingually (six languages, eight tokenizers), separation runs from 19/28 pairs (English) to 28/28 (German); English is the \emph{hardest} case, so English-only validation understates the method, and discrimination is best where GPU validation is least available.

\section{External validation on TokEval}
TokEval \cite{meister2026} releases 51 nanochat runs at ${\sim}$128K vocabulary spanning algorithms, pretokenizers, and training mixtures, with published val bpb. We reconstruct its training mixture from the paper's byte shares (36.9\% FineWeb-Edu, 33.4\% FineWeb2 over 30 languages, 16.1\% FineMath, 13.6\% code) and score every released tokenizer, training nothing. Held-out bigram bpb reaches $\rho{=}0.80$ ($p{=}7{\times}10^{-12}$), exceeding every intrinsic metric in TokEval's own tables (Table~\ref{tab:tokeval}). The contrast with plug-in trigram entropy (0.44, n.s.) is the informative one: similar sequential information, different estimator---held-out smoothed estimation with byte normalization is what does the work. On the mixture-matched subset, compression again drops to chance while the $n$-gram holds, reproducing the matched-panel finding externally at four times the size. But $n{=}3$ collapses to $\rho{=}0.05$ on the full panel, and the reason deserves its own section.

\section{The coverage confound: when more context inverts the ranking}
Consider a minimal pair from the TokEval panel: two BPE tokenizers with the \emph{same} algorithm, vocabulary size, and GPT-4o pretokenization regex, differing only in training data---the balanced multilingual mixture versus English-only web text (Table~\ref{tab:pair}). Downstream they are near-opposite ends of the panel: rank 2 versus rank 47 of 48. Held-out bigram bpb orders them correctly. Held-out \emph{trigram} bpb inverts them: the English-only tokenizer is scored 4th-best of 48.

The mechanism is visible in one word. The Russian \foreignlanguage{russian}{``государство''} is a single token under the balanced tokenizer and seven fragments (\foreignlanguage{russian}{г$\cdot$ос$\cdot$у$\cdot$д$\cdot$ар$\cdot$ств$\cdot$о}) under the English-only one. Fragments recur constantly, so the English-only model finds 51\% of its held-out trigrams verbatim in 30\,MB of training text and scores them with dense character-level statistics; the balanced tokenizer's whole-word trigrams are seen only 23\% of the time, so it backs off for three quarters of its predictions. The $n$-gram's context window is measured in \emph{tokens}: at fixed $n$, a fragmenting tokenizer buys coverage with the very fertility that makes it bad, and past some order the metric measures corpus coverage, not tokenizer quality. At $n{=}2$ the coverage gap is small (62\% vs.\ 80\%) and genuine modeling quality wins through; at $n{=}3$ the gap more than doubles and coverage dominates.

The matched panel of \S2 shows the opposite failure and completes the rule. There all nine arms have near-identical fertility, so trigram coverage is $\sim$37\% \emph{for every arm}: sparsity penalizes all candidates equally, cannot reorder them, and $n{\geq}3$ adds discriminating signal---while $n{=}2$, whose differences between near-identical arms are tiny, is the unstable order ($\tau{=}0.81$ across text samples; agreement 15/30, n.s.). Two failure modes therefore bracket the choice of $n$: too low is underpowered on closely matched candidates; too high conflates coverage with quality on fertility-heterogeneous ones. The prescription is operational, not aesthetic: \textbf{use the largest $n$ at which held-out $n$-gram coverage is comparable across candidates}. The coverage rates are computed by the metric itself, so the diagnostic is free, and a large coverage spread at some order is itself a warning that the panel mixes fertility regimes.

\section{Discussion}
Within its limits the method dominates its cost class: strictly stronger than compression and plug-in entropies on two independent testbeds, a fraction of a CPU-hour per tokenizer, precise enough for paired significance tests, and most discriminating in multilingual settings. Its profile matches what TokEval reports for its own predictor fits---screening, not selection: it flags every bad tokenizer (bottom-10 overlap with val bpb: 9/10) but does not order close leaders (top-10: 3/10), and on the matched panel it over-rates plain Unigram variants while placing the eventual CORE-best tokenizer 2nd of 10. It predicts language-modeling quality, not task accuracy---though at these scales task benchmarks cannot rank tokenizers either. \textbf{Limitations.} Downstream anchors come from one architecture family at ${\sim}$1.3B parameters; the TokEval corpus is a reconstruction of its mixture, not its validation shard; the Unigram-family bias on matched panels is observed but unexplained---an $n$-gram cannot exploit within-token composition, and embedding-based smoothing is the natural next probe. We release the implementation, both harnesses, and all per-document scores.
