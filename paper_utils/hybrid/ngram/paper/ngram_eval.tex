\begin{quote}\small\noindent\textbf{Abstract.}
Comparing tokenizers currently forces a choice between cheap intrinsic metrics with unreliable downstream correlation and GPU-scale pretraining sweeps. We propose a middle point: fit an interpolated modified Kneser--Ney $n$-gram language model to each tokenizer's own token stream and report held-out bits per UTF-8 byte (bpb), a family of measures that interpolates from intrinsic ($n{=}1$) toward extrinsic as $n$ grows, computable in CPU minutes. On nine closely matched 34.7K-vocabulary tokenizers with a 20-seed transformer sweep, where compression is at chance, $n$-gram bpb orders 85\% of the pairs both methods resolve ($p{=}0.0013$). On the 48-tokenizer TokEval suite at 128K vocabulary, held-out bigram bpb reaches Spearman $\rho{=}0.80$ against published 1.27B-model validation bpb, exceeding every intrinsic metric reported there, including R\'enyi efficiency (0.57) and plug-in trigram entropy (0.44, n.s.): held-out estimation and byte normalization, not sequential order per se, do the work. We characterize when the method fails, and show it discriminates best in exactly the multilingual settings where GPU validation is least affordable.
\end{quote}\vspace{6pt}}]

\section{Introduction}
Tokenizer evaluation is polarized. Intrinsic metrics---compression, fertility, R\'enyi efficiency \cite{zouhar2023}, Zipf statistics \cite{lotz2025}---cost seconds but correlate inconsistently with the models eventually trained \cite{schmidt2024,cognetta2024,ali2024}. Extrinsic evaluation pretrains a model per tokenizer \cite{meister2026,lotz2025}, which is exactly what practitioners iterating on a tokenizer cannot afford. Between plug-in corpus statistics and a transformer sits an old, unfashionable object: a smoothed $n$-gram language model. We show that, used carefully, it is a surprisingly strong tokenizer evaluation.

\textbf{Method.} For each candidate tokenizer we encode disjoint held-out and training text, fit an interpolated modified Kneser--Ney model \cite{chen1998} of order $n$ over the token stream (counts via dense-prefix-id \texttt{numpy}, no Python loop; minutes on a laptop), and report total held-out bits divided by the \emph{true UTF-8 byte length of the text}, measured before tokenization. Three design points matter. (i) The model is a proper distribution: level~1 interpolates into a uniform over the tokenizer's full alphabet, so bpb is a real code length and unused vocabulary is priced, not free. (ii) The true-byte denominator makes scores comparable across tokenizers without the per-token byte-accounting corrections neural bpb needs; a lossy tokenizer is flagged by a round-trip check rather than silently flattered. (iii) Because the metric is deterministic, uncertainty comes from a \emph{paired bootstrap over held-out documents}: every tokenizer scores the same documents, so document difficulty cancels and each pair of tokenizers gets a confidence interval. At $n{=}1$ the measure is close to token-entropy-per-byte intrinsic metrics; each increment adds sequential structure only a trained LM would otherwise see.

\section{Fine distinctions: a matched panel}
We first test the regime where intrinsic metrics are known to fail: nine tokenizers (BPE, Unigram, two BPE-initialized Unigram variants, MinGram, MinGram-PP, PathPiece at two prune settings, ConvexTok) sharing a 34.7K vocabulary, training corpus, and pretokenizer, each with $\geq$20 seeds of 1.27B-token nanochat \cite{karpathy2025} pretraining. Downstream val bpb separates 30 of 36 tokenizer pairs (Welch, $p{<}.05$); the task benchmark CORE separates only 9 of 36---its between-arm range is smaller than its seed noise, so at this scale \emph{the expensive benchmark is the one that cannot rank tokenizers}. The $n$-gram metric, from 5\,MB of held-out text, separates 31 of 36 pairs at 95\% and reproduces its ranking exactly on disjoint text at $n\geq3$ (Kendall $\tau{=}1.0$; $\tau{=}0.81$ below).

On the pairs downstream resolves, $n\geq3$ bpb agrees with val bpb on 21/30 ($p{=}.021$; disjoint replicate 20/30, $p{=}.049$); restricted to pairs where \emph{both} metrics are individually confident, agreement is 17/20 = 85\% ($p{=}.0013$). Compression agrees on 17/30 and 16/30---chance. The misses are systematic, not random: every disagreement over-rates a plain Unigram-family tokenizer relative to what the transformer rewards. Multilingually (six languages, eight tokenizers each), the metric separates from 19/28 pairs (English) to 28/28 (German): English is the \emph{hardest} case---arms sit $8\times$ closer there than in Finnish---so English-only validation understates the method, and discrimination is best precisely where GPU validation is least available. Bpb is not comparable across scripts (UTF-8 width), only within a language.

\section{External validation on TokEval}
TokEval \cite{meister2026} releases 51 nanochat runs at ${\sim}$128K vocabulary spanning algorithms, pretokenizers, and training mixtures, with published val bpb. We reconstruct its training mixture from the paper's byte shares (36.9\% FineWeb-Edu, 33.4\% FineWeb2 over 30 languages, 16.1\% FineMath, 13.6\% code) and score every released tokenizer, training nothing.

Held-out \emph{bigram} bpb reaches $\rho{=}0.80$ ($p{=}7{\times}10^{-12}$), exceeding every intrinsic metric in TokEval's own tables (Table~\ref{tab:tokeval}). The comparison with plug-in trigram entropy (0.44, not significant) is the informative one: similar sequential information, different estimator. Held-out smoothed estimation with byte normalization---not $n$-gram order---is what does the work. On the mixture-matched subset, compression again drops to chance while the $n$-gram holds, reproducing the matched-panel finding externally at four times the size.

The $n{=}3$ collapse on the full panel (+0.05) is diagnosable and instructive: English-only tokenizers fragment multilingual text into near-byte streams whose low-order statistics are dense, while 128K-vocabulary tokenizers' trigrams go sparse; the $n$-gram's context window is measured in \emph{tokens}, so fragmentation wins unfairly at higher order. Excluding mismatched tokenizers restores $n{=}3$ to $0.39$. Jointly with the matched panel (34.7K vocabulary, where $n\geq3$ was optimal and $n{=}2$ unstable), the prescription is that usable order scales with training tokens per vocabulary entry.

\section{Discussion}
The metric has the same profile TokEval reports for its own predictor fits: screening, not selection. It flags every bad tokenizer (bottom-10 overlap with val bpb: 9/10) but does not order close leaders (top-10: 3/10); on the matched panel it placed the eventual CORE-best tokenizer 2nd of 10 while over-rating plain Unigram variants. It predicts language-modeling quality, not task accuracy---though at the scales where cheap evaluation matters, task benchmarks cannot rank tokenizers either. Within those limits it dominates its cost class: strictly stronger than compression and plug-in entropies on two independent testbeds, a fraction of a CPU-hour per tokenizer, precise enough for paired significance tests, and most discriminating in multilingual settings. We release the implementation, both evaluation harnesses, and all per-document scores.

\textbf{Limitations.} Downstream anchors come from one architecture family (nanochat) at ${\sim}$1.3B parameters; the TokEval corpus is a reconstruction of its mixture, not its validation shard; and the systematic Unigram-family bias on matched panels is observed but not yet explained---an $n$-gram cannot exploit within-token composition, and embedding-based smoothing is the natural next probe.
